# Final Product Ontology Review

**Result stated first: the recommendation survives. Its justification does not.** My earlier derivation held that the Event File *is* an Atlas subject, citing Engagement L43. That was an over-read, and the challenge exposes it. Celebration grain and supersession survive on stronger ground.

---

## Part I — Beginning from reality

Set software aside. Ask what is in the world on 4 June.

Two people marry. Families gather in a hall. Food is eaten. Someone paid for it. Someone cooked it.

Those are not one thing. They are at least five, and they have different owners.

### Candidate 1 · The celebration

*The Goldstein wedding, as an occasion.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **1 · Independent of EventCore?** | **Yes.** It occurs whether or not any software exists |
| **2 · Two caterers recognise the same object?** | **Yes.** Both say *"the Goldstein wedding, June 4."* They agree on its identity while competing for it |

| **6 · Operational facts attached** | **None.** Our cooking is not a fact about their wedding |

### Candidate 2 · The gathering

*The Sunday seudah, at that hall, at that hour.*

Same profile: independent, mutually recognisable, family-owned. It is a constituent of the celebration, not a rival to it.

### Candidate 3 · The parties

*The couple, the hosts, the families.*

Independent, recognisable by anyone, and **owned by themselves**. What we hold are observations — *"the client's mother is coeliac"* — which the Knowledge Constitution places explicitly with Atlas as experience, superseded by later observation.

## Part II — The decisive test

Question 2 separates everything.

> **Two independent catering companies would recognise the same celebration. They would not recognise the same file.**

And the corpus supplies the matching test. Engagement L35 defines Atlas's domain as *"assertions about our own subjects — work, evidence, states, ownership, and experience"*, distinguished by: **"no counterparty; nothing bilateral; nobody outside can change it."**

Apply it to the celebration. **The family can move the date.** Someone entirely outside our institution can change the celebration without our participation or consent.

**The celebration therefore fails Atlas's defining property. It cannot be an Atlas subject.**

That is where my earlier derivation broke. I read Engagement L43 — *"Someone expressing interest undertakes nothing and obliges nobody. It is an assertion about our own subject — Atlas"* — as establishing that the celebration is an Atlas subject. It does not. It establishes that **the inquiry** is an Atlas fact. What the assertion is *about* is a different question, and L43 does not answer it.

Framework L319 does: *"A joint client with two signatories. The party's identity and composition is a subject we assert about — **Atlas**; the relation is **Engagement**."*

The client is **not ours**. Yet our identification of them is Atlas's. **Atlas owns the assertion, never the thing asserted about.**

## Part III — What, then, is the Event File?

Testing each model against the evidence above.

**Is it the real-world object?** **No.** We would own truth about something whose truth belongs to a family, and two caterers holding two files about one wedding would imply two weddings.

**Is it an institutional recognition of the object?** **Incomplete.** A recognition is a single act — *"this celebration exists and is this one."* The Event File is far more than that act; the recognition is only what identifies it.

**Is it an institutional dossier?** **Nearly right, and misleading.** "Dossier" implies a container of documents. But workspaces are lenses onto the file, not drawers within it, and the file holds no document of its own — it holds facts that live under other authorities.

**Is it an aggregate of several constitutional objects?** **No — and this is provable.** An aggregate has no identity beyond its members; regroup the members and nothing has been superseded. But merge and split require the prior identity to be **retained and to keep resolving**. There is nothing in an aggregate to retain. **Therefore the Event File has identity of its own.**

**Is it an institutional projection anchored to a real-world subject?** **Yes. This survives every test.**

### One software object need not correspond to one real-world object

The assumption fails in both directions, and the failures are ordinary.

**One real object, many institutional objects:** one celebration produces our file, a competitor's file, the venue's booking sheet, the photographer's job. Four institutional objects, one occasion. None is the occasion.

**One institutional object, many real objects:** our file spans a celebration, several gatherings, several payers, dozens of responsibilities. It corresponds to no single thing in the world.

**And the third case, which settles it:** an inquiry that never converts. We hold a file. **No celebration ever occurred.** The file is not incorrect and not empty — it truthfully records what we recognised, offered and were told. An object that can exist while its supposed referent never did is not that referent.

## Part IV — The anchor and the file

### The anchor object

**The celebration.** Real, external, owned by the family, not held by us, not governed by any of our jurisdictions — because it is not a truth but a subject that truths are about.

Critically: **the anchor is an identity criterion, not a held object.** We do not store the celebration. We use it to answer one question — *are these two things the same job?* — and nothing else. Without it, merge and split would have no criterion at all.

### The Event File

**The institutional object, wholly ours, identified by our recognition of one celebration, under which everything we observe, undertake and do concerning it is gathered.**

Its truth is entirely ours. Nobody outside can change it. The family may move the date, but that does not alter our file — it obliges us to **record a new observation**. Every change to the file is an append we make.

**That is why the Event File satisfies Atlas's test while its anchor does not.**

### Why they are not the same thing — five tests, each falsifiable

1. **Ownership of truth.** They own the celebration; we own the file.
2. **The file can be wrong.** They move the date and our file is now mistaken and must be corrected. **A thing cannot be wrong about itself.** This alone is sufficient.
3. **Multiplicity.** Two caterers, one wedding, two files. Identity would give two weddings.
4. **Partial footprint.** We cater three of seven sheva brachos. The celebration has seven nights; our file has three events. Identity would make our file 4/7 incomplete about itself. It is not incomplete — it is exact about our engagement.
5. **Merge and split are not events at the wedding.** When we merge two files, **nothing happened at the celebration.** We corrected an institutional error.

Point 5 supplies what was previously only asserted. The earlier recommendation chose supersession for merge and split by instinct. Now the reason is visible: **they are corrections to our recognition, not records of real-world change.** That is precisely why they cannot be lifecycle transitions, and why the prior identity must be retained — a corrected belief does not erase the belief that was held.

---

## Part V — Does the recommendation survive?

**Grain — survives, unchanged.** One Event File per celebration, engagements attaching many-to-one. The reasoning is now stronger: engagements are *our undertakings*, plural, all concerning one anchor. They were never properties of the celebration, so multi-payer sheva brachos is not an awkward case to accommodate — it is the ontology working as designed.

**Identity continuity — survives, and is now explained.** No lifecycle transition changes identity; only correction of a mis-identification does, by supersession with the prior identity retained and resolving. This is no longer a rule chosen for convenience but a consequence of the file being a recognition that can be wrong.

**Justification — fails and is replaced.** The Event File is **not** an Atlas subject. The celebration is not an Atlas subject either. **Our recognition of the celebration is the Atlas assertion, and the Event File is the institutional object that recognition identifies.**

**Nothing downstream changes.** All twenty-two edge cases stand. Several are now derived rather than enumerated: partial coverage follows from footprint (test 4); merge and split follow from correctability (test 5); the unconverted inquiry follows from the file's independence of its referent.

## Final ontology

| Layer | What it is | Whose truth | In a jurisdiction? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The celebration** | Real, external occasion | **The family's** | **No** — a subject, not a truth. Serves as our identity criterion |
| **Our recognition of it** | The assertion that a celebration exists and is this one | **Ours** | **Atlas** — correctable, superseded by later observation |
| **The Event File** | The institutional object that recognition identifies, under which everything concerning the celebration is gathered | **Ours, wholly** | Institutional. Holds facts governed elsewhere; **custody is not authority** |
| **Our undertakings** | Offers, options, terms, accounts — many per file | **Joint** | **Engagement** |
| **Our work** | Responsibilities, evidence, states, readiness | **Ours** | **Atlas** |
| **Our observations** | Experience about parties, venues, suppliers | **Ours** | **Atlas** as experience |

> **The Event File is an institutional projection whose identity is anchored to a real-world celebration it does not own, does not contain, and may be wrong about.**

The anchor gives it identity. The file gives it truth. They are different objects because one can be mistaken about the other — and that mistake, when corrected, is supersession.

**The recommendation stands. The ground beneath it has been replaced with sounder ground.**
